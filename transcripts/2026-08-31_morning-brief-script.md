In Central Ohio, Columbus City Council returns to a dense agenda where Flock cameras, data centers, utility trust, school finances, and growth pressure all meet operational governance. HOST: Good morning. It is Monday, August 31st, 2026, and this is your Morning Brief.

COHOST: The Monday pattern is escalation meeting operating limits. Oil markets, central banks, city councils, mortgage teams, and A I buyers all have to manage systems that are moving faster than their old controls.

HOST: Start nationally, with the Strait of Hormuz. AP reports U.S. forces struck Iranian rocket launchers near the strait on Sunday, the first U.S. military action in weeks after a brief lull in the war with Iran. Iran responded by firing missiles toward U.S. sites in Jordan, and Jordanian defenses intercepted them. On Monday, the United Arab Emirates said it intercepted an Iranian drone over its territorial waters.

COHOST: That is not just a foreign-policy story. It is an energy, inflation, insurance, shipping, and rate story.

HOST: Exactly. The affected parties are U.S. service members, Gulf allies, tanker operators, refiners, airlines, trucking companies, households filling gas tanks, and central bankers trying to decide whether inflation pressure is temporary or persistent. AP says oil prices surged nearly 3 percent in Asian trading, with Brent crude above 90 dollars a barrel and U.S. crude above 85 dollars. The practical consequence is that a supply-risk premium is back in the market before the September Fed meeting.

HOST: The counter-signal is that a price spike after a military exchange does not automatically become a sustained energy shock. Shipping can adjust, producers can increase supply, and diplomatic channels can reopen. But the risk is clear: if Iran keeps threatening traffic near Hormuz, investors may price higher energy costs into inflation, consumers may see fuel relief pushed farther away, and companies with fuel exposure may have to revisit margins.

COHOST: The watch item is whether the next move is military, diplomatic, or financial.

HOST: That is where Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent comes in. AP reports Bessent is heading into the G20 finance ministers' meeting in Asheville trying to rally allies behind Iran pressure while tariffs are already straining U.S. relationships with Canada, Europe, and China. He also told AP the U.S. plans to sanction another bank tied to Iran transactions, while leaving open the possibility of more action against Chinese entities that keep financing Iranian oil flows.

HOST: The useful interpretation is leverage. Military action can interrupt a threat, but sanctions and payment restrictions are meant to change the cost of doing business with Iran. The affected parties include banks, commodity traders, insurers, Chinese importers, Gulf states, European finance ministries, and U.S. consumers who ultimately feel oil prices. The uncertainty is coalition strength. If allies see U.S. tariffs as coercive, they may be less willing to follow Washington on sanctions. Watch for the named bank, any Chinese financial target, G20 language on Iran, and whether oil stays above 90 dollars after the first shock.

HOST: The Fed story now sits directly on top of that. AP reports Fed Chair Kevin Warsh used Jackson Hole to reopen the door to rate hikes if inflation does not cool. Friday's bond-market reaction pushed investors toward a higher probability of a September hike, and Monday's oil move complicates the inflation picture.

COHOST: For borrowers, the key detail is that the Fed is not just looking at one gasoline number.

HOST: Right. Warsh's argument is that underlying inflation has stayed too firm, and that A I productivity gains are not guaranteed to solve the price problem on the Fed's calendar. The affected parties are households with floating-rate debt, banks managing credit risk, small businesses, homebuilders, A I companies funding large capital spending, and local governments issuing debt. The practical consequence is planning discipline. Assume the September 15th and 16th F O M C meeting is live, but not decided.

HOST: The counter-signal is the labor market. If Friday's jobs report is weak enough, the Fed may decide that growth risk outweighs the oil and inflation risk. If jobs are solid and energy prices stay elevated, the case for a hike gets easier. Watch two-year Treasury yields, the 10-year, oil prices, the next inflation report, and whether other Fed officials echo Warsh or soften the message.

HOST: In Central Ohio, Columbus City Council comes back from summer recess with a heavy growth agenda. Axios Columbus reports council members are looking at Flock cameras, data centers, charter changes, youth programming, and broader service pressure as the city grows. That sounds like a list of separate issues, but the operating story is one thing: Columbus is trying to decide how much infrastructure, surveillance, energy use, and public-service demand it can absorb while still keeping residents' trust.

COHOST: Flock cameras remain the sharpest trust test because the city has already had to explain who touched the data and why.

HOST: Exactly. Mayor Andrew Ginther has proposed new rules for Columbus' 65 Flock automatic license plate readers, including seven-day retention, third-party analysis, tighter outside-agency access, supervisory review, immediate user suspension for suspected misuse, and a kill switch. Axios and W O S U have also framed the question council faces now: whether those promises become enforceable contract language and public audit practice.

HOST: The affected parties are drivers, police, crime victims, immigrant residents, civil-liberties groups, council members, and partner agencies. The practical consequence is procurement governance. If Columbus keeps the cameras, the real policy is not the press conference. It is the retention setting, the access log, the misuse penalty, the audit schedule, and the contract renewal language. The counter-signal is that police and some residents still argue the technology helps solve serious crimes and find missing people. Watch whether council publishes audit requirements and banned-use definitions before the end-of-year renewal.

HOST: Data centers are the quieter but bigger resource question. W O S U reported in July that Columbus City Council is pursuing regulations rather than a ban, including water and sewer reporting and a decommissioning plan if a facility closes. Earlier local reporting said the region already had roughly 130 data centers and dozens more proposed. For Central Ohio households, this is not only an A I story. It is a power, water, tax-incentive, land-use, and neighborhood-noise story.

COHOST: The important local tradeoff is that A I growth can look like jobs and tax base from one angle, and utility load from another.

HOST: Exactly. The practical consequence is that council needs rules that make project costs visible before approval: expected water draw, grid upgrades, sewer capacity, emergency backup systems, local tax treatment, and what happens if the operator leaves. The counter-signal is that outright bans can push projects into suburbs with fewer controls while still affecting regional utilities. Watch for draft ordinance text, utility-capacity disclosures, and whether Columbus coordinates with suburbs instead of regulating parcel by parcel.

HOST: The third local pressure point is public finance. Ten T V and W O S U reported this month that Columbus City Schools is projecting a deficit of about 53 million dollars this fiscal year even after major cuts last year. District leaders have warned that without new revenue or more cuts, the path could include additional staff reductions, closures, or a levy conversation.

COHOST: That matters for more than parents. School finances shape neighborhoods, property taxes, housing choices, and employer confidence.

HOST: Right. The affected parties are students, teachers, families, taxpayers, city leaders, and buyers comparing districts in a high-rate housing market. The practical consequence is that Columbus residents should listen for specifics: whether the district is solving a one-year budget gap or a structural mismatch between costs and revenue. The counter-signal is that forecasts can move with state funding, enrollment, labor costs, and local tax collections. Watch the next board budget discussion, any levy timeline, and whether proposed cuts are tied to a multi-year academic plan rather than a spreadsheet exercise.

HOST: In home lending and mortgage, the fresh rate picture remains stubborn. Freddie Mac says the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage averaged 6.66 percent as of August 27th, barely up from 6.65 the prior week and near the year's highs. The 15-year averaged 5.98 percent. M B A says mortgage applications fell 1 percent in the week ending August 21st. Bankrate's Monday average is still in the high sixes.

COHOST: The useful lender message is that stable does not mean easy.

HOST: Exactly. A rate that sits near 6.7 percent for weeks can still drain demand because the payment shock is already baked in. The affected parties are buyers trying to qualify, refinancers waiting for a real savings window, loan officers, real estate agents, builders, servicers, credit unions, and secondary-market desks. The practical consequence is segmentation. Purchase teams need to know which borrowers can move with seller credits, builder buydowns, down-payment help, or debt reduction, and which borrowers need a lower price point or more time. Refinance teams should focus on borrowers who are truly in the money after costs, not broad marketing blasts to everyone with rate frustration.

HOST: For lenders, the operating issue is pipeline honesty. Rate locks need tighter communication because September data can move Treasury yields quickly. Pre-approvals need payment stress tests because insurance, taxes, and H O A costs can break affordability even when the note rate is known. Servicing teams should identify borrowers vulnerable to competitor outreach if rates dip even modestly.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that a weaker jobs report could pull long rates lower even if the Fed stays hawkish.

HOST: Right. Mortgages do not mechanically follow the Fed funds rate. They move with the 10-year Treasury, inflation expectations, M B S spreads, credit risk, and lender capacity. Watch the September 3rd Freddie Mac release, the next M B A purchase-refinance split, jobs data on Friday, builder incentive language, and whether mortgage-backed spreads widen if markets get nervous about oil and inflation.

HOST: The A I implementation angle in lending is becoming more practical. In a high-rate market, the value is not replacing judgment. It is helping teams find the next best action. That means document intelligence for missing-file detection, borrower-readiness scoring, condition summaries, compliant follow-up drafts, servicing-retention alerts, and quality-control checks before a file becomes expensive rework.

COHOST: The governance model matters because lending is regulated and explainable.

HOST: Exactly. The human stays in the loop for suitability, fair-lending risk, exceptions, adverse-action explanations, pricing, and final borrower communication. The proof is lower cost per loan, shorter cycle time, higher pull-through, fewer defects, and better retention. The warning sign is an A I pilot that produces impressive summaries but never connects to the loan origination system, servicing system, compliance evidence, or a measurable operating metric.

HOST: In broader A I and technology, the latest-models story is about capability becoming cheaper, faster, and harder to govern. OpenAI says it reduced GPT-5.6 Sol A P I and credit pricing by more than 20 percent through at least late November, and its model documentation now lists lower per-token prices for Sol. Google says Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash is production-ready in the Gemini A P I with stronger video editing controls, first-and-last-frame control, scene extension, draft previews, and 4K output. Anthropic opened a research preview of its Model Hardware Standard for agents that operate physical devices.

COHOST: That combination is important: cheaper reasoning, better media control, and agents moving closer to real equipment.

HOST: Exactly. The affected parties are developers, banks, marketing teams, compliance departments, manufacturers, schools, insurers, and procurement leaders. The practical consequence is that enterprises need a model control plane, not a model wishlist. They need routing by task, private evaluations, approved data paths, access scopes, audit logs, cost budgets, human approval points, and incident response.

HOST: For financial services and home lending, the use cases are concrete: research, document review, coding, analytics, sales enablement, compliance monitoring, customer support, underwriting support, and servicing triage. Lower model prices can make high-volume workflows more viable, but they can also tempt teams to automate before controls are ready. Video models can speed training, customer education, and marketing production, but brand review and disclosure rules still apply. Physical-device standards matter because agents that can operate lab equipment, factory systems, or robotics create a different risk class than chatbots.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that announcements are not deployment evidence.

HOST: Right. A product release does not prove reliability inside a regulated workflow. Watch customer case studies with measurable outcomes, model-risk guidance, vendor audit features, agent registries, and whether companies can show what an agent did, what data it touched, who approved it, and how it can be stopped.

HOST: Markets start the week with investors balancing oil risk against rate risk. AP says Asian shares were mixed Monday as oil jumped after the U.S. strike near Hormuz. U.S. futures edged lower. On Friday, A I-linked leadership had already cooled, and Nvidia sold off after earlier earnings strength. The market assumption is that A I spending remains powerful, but the cost of capital and energy shock risk are harder to ignore.

COHOST: That makes this a breadth test, not just a mega-cap A I test.

HOST: Exactly. Watch whether money rotates into defensive sectors, energy, banks, or cash-like yields, and whether small caps can hold up if the two-year yield keeps pricing Fed pressure. What could weaken the cautious view is a softer oil market, better inflation data, and a jobs report that cools without signaling recession. What could strengthen it is Brent staying above 90 dollars, higher short-term yields, or earnings guidance that shows companies delaying purchases because financing costs are too high.

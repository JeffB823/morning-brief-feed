TITLE: Morning Brief for Friday, July 31, 2026
DESC: Today's brief leads with a mixed U.S. economy: second-quarter growth slowed to 1.5 percent, consumers kept spending, A I investment stayed strong, and inflation remained above the Fed's target just after a divided rate hold. In Central Ohio, Ohio's midterm ad market is becoming a local operating story for Columbus voters, media, campaigns, and civic attention. In home lending, Freddie Mac's 30-year mortgage rate rose to 6.66 percent, the highest level in a year, forcing lenders back into rate-lock, concession, and borrower-segmentation discipline. In A I and technology, Google DeepMind's Gemini Robotics 2 moves the model race into physical work, while Scale A I's new enterprise-focused CEO shows where the implementation layer is heading. Plus markets and Columbus weather.

HOST: Good morning. It is Friday, July 31st, 2026, and this is your Morning Brief.

COHOST: The frame today is proof under pressure. The economy has growth, but not clean growth. A I has capability, but not clean control. Housing has inventory, but not cheap financing. And Ohio politics has attention, but not much quiet.

HOST: Start with the national economy, because yesterday's data gave the Fed a complicated picture, not a simple green light. The Associated Press reports the U.S. economy grew at a 1.5 percent annual pace in the second quarter. That is slower than the 2.1 percent pace in the first quarter and below expectations. But the details are less weak than the headline: consumer spending rose at a 3.2 percent annual rate, and a measure of underlying domestic demand grew 3.9 percent.

COHOST: So the headline says slowdown, but the inside of the report says households and businesses are still moving.

HOST: Exactly. The drag was imports. Imports rose at an 11.5 percent pace, and because G D P counts domestic production, imports subtract from the number. AP says part of that import surge came from chips and other products supporting A I investment. Business investment outside housing was still strong, up at an 8.4 percent pace. That matters because A I is no longer just a stock-market theme. It is showing up as real capital spending, supply-chain demand, data-center construction, power demand, and imported hardware.

HOST: The practical consequence is that policymakers and executives cannot treat this as a normal soft-growth report. Consumers are still spending. Businesses are still investing. But the economy is absorbing higher borrowing costs, higher energy risk from the Iran war, and still-elevated prices.

COHOST: The counter-signal is inflation. The P C E price index eased from May, but it did not get close to the Fed's 2 percent target.

HOST: Right. The Commerce Department's P C E index rose 3.7 percent from a year earlier in June, down from 4.1 percent in May. Core P C E was 3.3 percent, little changed from 3.4 percent. Prices fell month to month because gasoline and other energy products dropped in June, but that relief came before the newest Iran escalation and oil rebound. That is why the Fed's Wednesday hold still matters this morning. The target range stayed at 3.5 to 3.75 percent, but three Fed officials dissented in favor of a hike.

HOST: Who is affected? Households with grocery, gas, rent, and credit-card exposure. Employers planning wages. Retailers watching real spending power. Builders and lenders watching rates. And investors trying to decide whether A I capital spending can offset inflation pressure. What could weaken the hawkish interpretation is another clean inflation report and lower oil. What strengthens it is energy passing through to goods, freight, airfare, and expectations. Watch July jobs, oil, and Fed speakers before Jackson Hole.

HOST: In Washington, one political development sharpens the same national story. The Senate narrowly rejected a war-powers effort to halt Trump's Iran hostilities, with the resolution failing 49 to 50. That does not end the conflict, and it does not end congressional pressure. It shows the administration still has room to operate, but the margin is thin enough that every oil spike, attack on U.S. forces, or new cost estimate can change the politics quickly.

COHOST: The useful takeaway is that the Iran story now has three clocks: the military clock, the energy-price clock, and the congressional clock.

HOST: Exactly. The next watch item is whether the White House can define an end state before lawmakers, voters, or fuel prices define one for it.

HOST: In Central Ohio, the freshest local operating story is political attention and money. Axios Columbus reports political groups are expected to spend about 750 million dollars in Ohio this election cycle, according to AdImpact's 2026 report. That is 70 percent above the initial forecast and puts Ohio fourth nationally in projected ad spending, behind California, Michigan, and Texas.

COHOST: That matters locally because Columbus is not just watching the midterms. It is one of the places where turnout, messaging, and media saturation will be fought hardest.

HOST: Right. Axios notes Ohio's three C cities are the largest Democratic strongholds, and both parties see major races here: Sherrod Brown against appointed Republican Senator Jon Husted, Amy Acton against Vivek Ramaswamy for governor, and redrawn House races that could affect control of Congress. For Columbus listeners, the practical consequence is not only more commercials. It is pressure on local news inventory, digital ad costs, campaign field hiring, ballot education, and voter fatigue right as local tax issues and public-safety debates also need attention.

HOST: The counter-signal is that ad spending does not equal persuasion. It can mobilize, but it can also numb voters. The more operational watch item is turnout. Axios points to May primary turnout where the parties nearly matched after Republican voters had outnumbered Democrats almost two to one in the 2022 May primary. Watch whether Columbus and Franklin County become a turnout engine, whether the October 5 registration deadline becomes a major organizing marker, and whether local ballot questions get drowned out by national messaging.

HOST: Also keep the Columbus civic calendar in view. The Ohio State Fair is underway, and Axios reports two Freedom 250 attractions from the Trump administration's national initiative are coming to the fair: the Freedom Ferris Wheel and mobile museum trucks. That is lighter than tax policy, but it fits the same attention story. The fair is a family event, a traffic event, and this year also a national-message event. The practical note is to expect crowds around the fairgrounds and more political texture than usual.

HOST: In home lending and mortgage, the fresh number is Freddie Mac's Thursday update. The 30-year fixed-rate mortgage averaged 6.66 percent as of July 30, up from 6.58 percent the prior week. The 15-year averaged 6.04 percent, up from 5.96 percent. Freddie Mac says the housing market continues to benefit from more available inventory, but the rate move puts that inventory into a harder affordability box.

COHOST: That is the mortgage tension right now: more choices for buyers, but a payment calculation that keeps getting less forgiving.

HOST: Exactly. This is the highest 30-year Freddie Mac average in a year, and it follows the M B A report showing applications fell 6.4 percent last week. For borrowers, the operational issue is not whether 6.66 sounds high in the abstract. It is whether the loan still qualifies after taxes, insurance, dues, points, and cash to close. For loan officers, the issue is pipeline triage. Who needs a lock now? Who needs seller credits? Who can use builder incentives? Who should compare temporary buydowns against permanent points? And who is really a nurture lead until rates or income change?

HOST: The counter-signal is inventory. More listings can create room for concessions, and some households can still transact if sellers participate in the financing solution. But higher Treasury yields and a divided Fed reduce the chance of quick rate relief. The watch item is whether the 10-year Treasury cools after the P C E report, whether lenders keep spreads tight to defend volume, and whether next week's M B A report shows buyers adapting or stepping back.

COHOST: For mortgage leaders, this is also a communication test. Vague refinance promises are weaker than a written trigger tied to rate, payment, and break-even math.

HOST: In A I and technology, the newest model story is physical. Google DeepMind introduced Gemini Robotics 2, a model family for whole-body robot control, fine dexterity, and multi-robot teamwork. Google says the system is meant to move robots beyond tabletop demonstrations toward coordinated movement from feet to fingertips. Reporting from W I R E D says the system combines models that understand vision and language with vision-language-action models that control full-body movement, hands, and grippers.

COHOST: That changes the enterprise question. A chatbot can make a bad recommendation. A robot can move through a warehouse, clinic, lab, store, or home.

HOST: Exactly. The practical workflows are not here for every company tomorrow, but the direction matters: warehouse handling, inspection, light manufacturing, elder-care assistance, facilities work, lab operations, and defense logistics. The implementation model is very different from deploying a document copilot. Companies need physical safety boundaries, human proximity detection, task approvals, environment mapping, maintenance controls, incident logs, and insurance review. Google is also introducing an ASIMOV-Agentic benchmark for robot safety, which is the right kind of signal: capability releases need evaluation systems that match the risk surface.

HOST: The counter-signal is that demonstrations are still demonstrations. W I R E D notes the models are trained through simulations, video examples, and human teleoperation, and robots still need specific training for complex real-world tasks. The next watch item is access: which developers can use the models, which robot bodies can run them reliably, and whether safety benchmarks catch enough edge cases before robots work around people.

HOST: The second A I signal is less flashy but just as practical. Axios reports Scale A I hired Google Cloud C O O Francis deSouza as chief executive. Scale built its business on data and human evaluations for model training, but Axios says the company is expanding into enterprise and government A I applications, and its applications business is expected to overtake its data business within 18 months.

COHOST: That is a useful enterprise tell. The money is moving from training data alone toward deployment infrastructure, evaluation, and business outcomes.

HOST: Exactly. For banks, mortgage companies, insurers, and large employers, the takeaway is that A I value is migrating into the operating layer: private evaluations, workflow integration, governance evidence, human review queues, model routing, data permissions, and measurable cost or cycle-time improvement. The risk is vendor sprawl. Every platform will claim it can turn pilots into production. The buyer's job is to ask which system of record it touches, what data it needs, who owns failure, and what metric improves.

HOST: Markets gave A I investors a rebound, but not a clean all-clear. AP reports the S and P 500 rose 1.7 percent Thursday, the Dow gained 613 points, or 1.2 percent, and the Nasdaq jumped 2.8 percent. Microsoft rose 15.5 percent, its best day since 2008, after earnings reassured investors about cloud growth and A I spending. Chip names rebounded too.

COHOST: But the bond market did not give the same relaxed signal.

HOST: Right. Long-term inflation worries remain, and the rally was still heavily tied to a few A I leaders. This morning, futures looked higher after Amazon's strong results, while Apple weakened after guidance concerns. The assumption being priced is that A I capex can produce enough revenue to justify the buildout. What could weaken that assumption is higher long-term yields, oil pressure, narrow market breadth, or earnings that show spending rising faster than useful demand.

Saturday turns humid, not as warm, and brings the bigger planning risk: a heavy thunderstorm in the afternoon. Sunday stays humid with showers and a thunderstorm possible.

Two, whether Ohio's record ad market turns Columbus into a turnout engine or just a saturated media market. Three, whether mortgage lenders can convert borrowers with concessions and lock discipline while the 30-year sits at 6.66 percent. Four, whether Google can turn robotics safety benchmarks into deployable controls. Five, whether Scale A I's enterprise turn signals a broader shift from model demos to governed production. And six, whether the market's A I rebound broadens beyond the biggest winners.

COHOST: The common thread is that the easy headline is less useful than the operating test: who pays, who controls the risk, who can measure the result, and what changes next.

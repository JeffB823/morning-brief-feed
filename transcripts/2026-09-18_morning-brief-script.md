TITLE: Early Voting, Data Center Rates, Seven-Percent Mortgages, and AI Self-Improvement - September 18, 2026
DESC: Early voting begins under existing state systems while federal pressure on election administration continues; Columbus weighs data-center utility guardrails; Freddie Mac puts the 30-year mortgage average at 6.95 percent; JPMorgan moves Claude use toward sandboxed controls; Anthropic says Claude now leads a quarter of its model R&D; markets and Columbus weather stay compact.
HOST: Today: early voting turns election lawsuits into live operating rules, Columbus takes a utility-cost swing at data centers, mortgage rates brush seven percent, and two AI stories show the same theme from opposite ends: banks are containing agents while labs let them build more AI.
HOST: Good morning. This is your Morning Brief for Friday, September 18th, 2026.
CHAPTER: National
HOST: Start nationally with elections, because the calendar changed this morning. AP reports in-person voting for the midterms begins today in Virginia, while voters in Idaho, Minnesota and South Dakota can cast absentee ballots in person, usually at local election offices or voting centers.
COHOST: After this week's mail-ballot ruling, what changes for election officials now that voting is actually underway?
HOST: The story moves from litigation to execution. The Supreme Court left lower-court blocks in place against the Postal Service mail-ballot plan, but AP says other federal efforts continue, including court challenges, searches for noncitizen voters and pressure on states to share sensitive voter data.
COHOST: So voters should not hear "court fight" and assume their county office has new instructions this morning.
HOST: Correct. The useful action is to check the official state or county election site before voting, especially if you plan to vote early or by mail. Election administrators say they are prepared, but AP notes voter-rights groups are still encouraging early ballots partly because of concern about Postal Service processing delays.
COHOST: What is the operating risk now?
HOST: Confusion. Early voting is supposed to reduce Election Day lines and give officials more time to process ballots. If voters absorb national arguments as if they are local rule changes, county workers have to spend scarce time correcting rumors instead of moving people through the system.
COHOST: That also means local election offices become the trusted source, not the loudest national voice. A voter who confirms location, identification rules and ballot deadlines now gives the system more room to handle surprises later.
HOST: The broader national point is that the midterms are no longer a future political season. The next six weeks will test voting operations, redrawn districts, affordability anxiety, the Iran war and AI worries at the same time.
CHAPTER: Columbus
HOST: In Columbus, the fresh local operating story is data-center utility cost. Axios Columbus reports Council President Shannon Hardin and Councilmember Christopher Wyche are backing new guardrails for future data center projects, and the first ordinance is already on the September 28 council agenda.
COHOST: What would that ordinance actually do?
HOST: The city file says it would require Columbus Water and Power to establish peak rates or other fees for large load and high-volume industrial and manufacturing users, including data centers. The point is cost causation: big, variable users should pay for infrastructure they force the system to build.
COHOST: That sounds broader than an anti-data-center measure.
HOST: It is written more broadly. The file mentions chip fabrication, brewing and food processing too. But data-center growth is the pressure point, and the legislation says Central Ohio has more than one hundred operational data centers, with close to two hundred statewide.
COHOST: Why does a water-and-power rate design matter to a resident who never sees the server building?
HOST: Because the question is who pays for capacity. If a new 48-million-gallon-per-day Home Road Water Plant or power infrastructure supports peak industrial demand, residents and small businesses do not want the cost folded quietly into ordinary bills. The package also points toward renewable-energy requirements, water-use disclosure, reclaimed-water encouragement and decommissioning plans.
COHOST: The tradeoff is that Central Ohio wants the jobs and tax base, but residents want proof the upside is not subsidized through utility bills.
HOST: Exactly. Watch the September 28 vote and whether Columbus Water and Power comes back with a concrete rate design rather than a slogan.
COHOST: The useful test is transparency. If council wants compromise instead of backlash, residents need to see the demand charge, capacity assumption and environmental conditions before the next project asks for approvals.
CHAPTER: Home Lending
HOST: In home lending, the operational change is borrower requalification under a new weekly benchmark. Freddie Mac says the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage averaged 6.95 percent as of September 17, up from 6.76 percent one week earlier. The 15-year rose to 6.26 percent.
COHOST: We have talked about high daily pricing all week. What is different when Freddie's survey catches up?
HOST: The public reference point moves. A buyer who heard a 6.76 percent national average last week may now hear almost seven percent from Freddie Mac itself. That affects preapproval refreshes, seller-credit negotiations, builder incentives, buydown math and lock counseling.
COHOST: Which loan files should lenders pull first this morning?
HOST: Active purchase files with thin debt-to-income margins, expiring locks, contracts using seller concessions and borrowers who were shopping based on stale payment assumptions. The affected workflow is not only rate quoting; it is deciding whether the approval letter, cash-to-close and monthly payment still match the offer.
COHOST: Does the Fed hike itself cause this, or is that too simple?
HOST: Too simple. AP notes the Fed controls short rates, while mortgages are tied more closely to long-term Treasury yields and investor demand for mortgage-backed securities. The Fed's anti-inflation move helped calm the 10-year yield on Thursday, but Freddie's weekly survey still reflects the rate pressure already in the market.
COHOST: So the consequence is discipline, not panic.
HOST: Right. Loan officers should stop using last week's payment as a placeholder. Borrowers should ask whether the quote includes taxes, insurance, dues, points, credits and a real lock option. The next watch is whether post-Fed long yields ease enough to pull daily mortgage pricing back below the new weekly headline.
COHOST: And for builders or agents, a concession is only useful if it solves the actual file. A price cut, a rate buydown and a seller credit can have very different effects on approval.
CHAPTER: AI in Banking
HOST: For AI in Banking, I changed the JPMorganChase ledger row. Business Insider reports the bank is putting some Claude use behind a 2,000-dollar monthly spending limit and a broader security architecture called Devspace.
COHOST: What changed in the row: usage, cost control, or security?
HOST: Security and internal-productivity controls. The task lane is software engineering and internal productivity. Developers had piloted Claude directly on desktops; Devspace is described as a containerized, sandbox-like server in Amazon Web Services that restricts Claude's access to employee credentials and internal systems.
COHOST: What is the evidence rank?
HOST: Reputable reporting, with named comments from JPMorgan's security leadership. The useful numbers are scope, not outcome metrics: Business Insider says an internal dashboard showed about 8,000 Claude licenses in a 65,000-person technology division, and a Devspace Teams group had about 1,900 members in early September. There is no disclosed metric for code quality, defect reduction or developer hours saved.
COHOST: What would this change at a large bank?
HOST: It changes the approval path for coding assistants. Instead of asking only whether Claude makes engineers faster, a bank asks whether the assistant has its own identity, no standing entitlements and no direct path to customer data, credentials or internal systems.
COHOST: The limitation is that a sandbox does not prove the code is good.
HOST: Exactly. It is a containment control, not a productivity study. The next upgrade would be a bank-disclosed comparison of speed, defects, security findings and review time after engineers move into the sandboxed workflow.
CHAPTER: Frontier AI
HOST: On the frontier, Anthropic disclosed a self-improvement metric that is more concrete than another warning speech. AP reports Claude is now leading 26 percent of Anthropic's model research and development, meaning it can complete most of a task end to end from a high-level prompt while still under human supervision.
COHOST: What bar does that clear for the frontier segment?
HOST: Agent and tool-use advances, plus a safety measurement. Anthropic says about 90 percent of its research and development is now done in collaboration with Claude, and AP reports roughly 30,000 agents were doing research and engineering work as of August.
COHOST: Is that recursive self-improvement already?
HOST: Anthropic says no. The model is not working completely autonomously, and AP says it is unclear how close the company thinks it is to that threshold. The measured part is the disclosed share of model R&D led or assisted by Claude. The claimed lesson is that labs should publish comparable methods so outsiders can judge whether AI is accelerating AI development.
COHOST: Who is advantaged if this keeps moving?
HOST: Frontier labs with strong internal agent platforms and oversight systems. The risk is that progress becomes harder for outsiders to measure because the tools building the next model are partly hidden inside the lab. For a regulated bank, the earliest use is not self-improving AI; it is asking vendors for comparable agent-oversight metrics before approving autonomous development tools on sensitive code.
COHOST: That bridges back to JPMorgan nicely. The lab is measuring how much work agents can lead. The bank is asking how little authority an agent can have while still helping engineers.
HOST: That distinction matters. Capability growth is exciting at the lab. Inside a regulated firm, the safer milestone is useful work with bounded authority, monitored actions and a human still accountable for the decision.

That is the combination borrowers and builders wanted to see: lower oil pressure and a calmer long bond. The caution is that Freddie's rate survey still moved up sharply, so one better market day does not repair affordability.
COHOST: The test is follow-through, especially if investors keep worrying about inflation, federal debt and AI-related borrowing.
HOST: Right. Watch whether the long yield stays below five percent and whether mortgage-backed securities improve enough for lenders to pass relief into rate sheets.

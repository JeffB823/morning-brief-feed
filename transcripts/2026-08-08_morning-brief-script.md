The unemployment rate edged down to 4.1 percent, but that is not a clean strength signal because the labor force also shrank by 264,000 people.

COHOST: So the headline unemployment rate improved, but the practical labor-market story weakened.

HOST: Exactly. B L S also revised May and June lower by a combined 103,000 jobs. That means the slowdown was not just a one-month miss. Local government education lost 50,000 jobs, retail lost 19,000, and financial activities continued to trend down. Health care still added jobs, but at a slower pace than its twelve-month average. Average hourly earnings were little changed for the month and up 3.2 percent over the year.

HOST: The affected parties are workers, employers, school systems, retailers, banks, borrowers, investors, and the Fed. The practical consequence is planning under softer demand. Employers have another reason to delay hiring. Workers see fewer openings even if layoffs are not exploding. Borrowers get a possible rate break, but only if bond yields stay lower and inflation does not re-accelerate.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that the payroll estimate has a wide confidence range, and July education numbers can be messy because of seasonal adjustment.

HOST: Right. The interpretation should be cautious, not dramatic. But the revisions matter because they say prior months were weaker than first reported. Watch next week's inflation data, jobless claims, and the August jobs report on September 4th. If softer jobs meet cooler inflation, the Fed has room to wait. If energy and prices stay hot, the Fed faces the harder problem: a labor market losing momentum while inflation pressure remains uncomfortable.

HOST: The second national story is a court ruling that sounds symbolic but is really about who authorizes federal construction. A divided D C Circuit panel ruled Friday that the Trump administration must stop aboveground construction on the 400 million dollar White House ballroom because Congress has not approved the project.

COHOST: That makes this less about whether a ballroom is a good idea and more about whether private fundraising can bypass congressional authorization on federal property.

HOST: Exactly. AP reports the proposed ballroom would be about 90,000 square feet where the East Wing stood before Trump ordered its demolition last fall. The administration argues the broader project includes security, medical, bunker, and military facilities. The court allowed underground security work to continue, but said aboveground ballroom construction needs congressional authorization. The ruling is stayed for two weeks so the administration can appeal.

HOST: The affected parties are Congress, the White House, preservation groups, federal planners, contractors, donors, and future administrations. The practical consequence is precedent. If the president can transform major White House structures using private money and executive direction, Congress loses leverage over public space, oversight, and appropriations. If the courts hold the line, even privately funded presidential projects need statutory authority when they alter federal property.

COHOST: The uncertainty is the Supreme Court. A two-week stay means the practical answer is not final.

HOST: Watch whether the administration files quickly, whether the justices let construction resume while the appeal proceeds, and whether Congress moves from complaint to explicit authorization or prohibition.

HOST: In Central Ohio, Monday's Columbus City Council hearing on Flock license-plate cameras now has a sharper fact pattern. W O S U reports two former Reynoldsburg police officers may have made dozens of personal searches on Flock cameras. One allegedly searched the same plate 46 times from December through June, much of it while on medical leave. Another allegedly searched one plate 53 times between January and June.

COHOST: That changes the hearing. It is not only about outside agencies and immigration searches. It is about internal misuse by people with access.

HOST: Exactly. Columbus was already under pressure after an audit found more than 15,000 possible immigration-related searches in its Flock system and Mayor Andrew Ginther ordered police to stop statewide sharing. Reynoldsburg adds the personal-use risk. License-plate readers can help investigations, but they also create a searchable movement history. The question for Monday is whether Columbus has rules that prevent misuse before a scandal, or only logs that explain misuse afterward.

HOST: The affected parties are drivers, police, immigrants, crime victims, neighborhoods, city council, prosecutors, and outside agencies. The practical consequence is governance design. A serious policy needs a narrow list of qualifying crimes, clear search-purpose requirements, limits on outside sharing, immigration-use restrictions, retention limits, random audits, public reports, supervisor review for sensitive searches, and consequences for improper access.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that Reynoldsburg says an audit found the alleged behavior. That means logs can work if audits are routine and independent.

HOST: Right. Watch Monday's hearing for whether council treats Flock as a contract-management problem, a civil-liberties problem, or a police-capacity problem. The most useful answer will probably have to be all three.

HOST: Also local and practical: Ohio's back-to-school sales tax holiday is underway through Sunday night, but it is narrower than last year's expanded version. The Ohio Department of Taxation says the exemption covers clothing priced at 75 dollars or less per item, school supplies at 20 dollars or less, and school instructional materials at 20 dollars or less.

COHOST: So for families, the planning point is item-by-item eligibility. This is not the broad under-500-dollar holiday people may remember.

HOST: Exactly. For Central Ohio households, the savings are real but targeted. Shoes, uniforms, notebooks, binders, and basic supplies may qualify. Many electronics, higher-priced clothes, sports gear, and household purchases will not. The operational advice is to check receipts before leaving the store, separate qualifying from nonqualifying items online, and avoid buying something unnecessary just because the tax is waived.

HOST: In home lending and mortgage, the fresh issue is the collision between high mortgage rates and weaker jobs. Freddie Mac says the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage averaged 6.69 percent as of August 6th, up from 6.66 percent the prior week and 6.63 percent a year ago. The 15-year rate fell slightly to 6.01 percent. M B A said earlier this week that applications dropped 2.9 percent for the week ending July 31st.

COHOST: But Friday's jobs report may change the borrower conversation more than the weekly rate average did.

HOST: Exactly. Freddie Mac's survey reflects applications submitted through Wednesday. After Friday's weaker jobs data, Treasury yields fell and stocks rallied because investors saw a lower chance that the Fed would move aggressively toward higher rates. For lenders, the practical move is not to promise relief. It is to refresh scenarios. Purchase borrowers need updated payment, cash-to-close, seller-credit, buydown, and debt-to-income numbers. Refinance prospects need written trigger rates and break-even math, not a vague check-in.

HOST: The affected parties are borrowers, loan officers, real estate agents, builders, servicers, secondary desks, credit unions, banks, and independent mortgage banks. The counter-signal is inflation. If next week's price data or oil prices heat back up, the rate relief from a soft jobs report can disappear quickly.

COHOST: And there is a mortgage A I governance point this week too.

HOST: Yes. Fannie Mae's A I and machine-learning governance framework took effect 120 days after its April 8th lender letter, which puts the effective date this week. The letter says seller-servicers using A I or machine learning in origination or servicing need policies, risk management, ownership, information-security alignment, and vendor controls no less protective than Fannie Mae's expectations. Upon request, they must be ready to disclose what A I they use, the purpose, the manner of use, and safeguards.

HOST: The practical implication for mortgage executives is evidence. A chatbot, document-intelligence tool, pricing model, quality-control assistant, or servicing agent now needs an owner, policy, audit trail, vendor oversight, human review where needed, and a way to prove compliance. The watch item is whether lenders can map actual tools to actual loan processes before investors or regulators ask.

HOST: In A I and technology, the latest model story is not only a release note. OpenAI said Friday that internal evaluations of an upcoming model called Astra show advances in agentic coding and cybersecurity strong enough that the company cannot rule out critical cyber capabilities under its Preparedness Framework. OpenAI says previous models, including GPT-5.6 Sol, were assessed at the high threshold rather than critical.

COHOST: That is a serious enterprise signal. The model may still be upcoming, but the control layer has to be designed before deployment, not after.

HOST: Exactly. OpenAI says it is scaling robustness testing, using isolated testing environments, restricting network and tool access, strengthening weight protections, adding monitoring, pausing internal Astra work that does not meet the new controls, and working with government agencies and selected safety organizations. The company also said Astra was not involved in the Hugging Face security incident.

HOST: For banks, mortgage companies, insurers, software firms, and large enterprises, the practical lesson is that agent governance has to move from policy language to architecture. If an A I system can browse, write code, call tools, open tickets, touch repositories, read customer records, or trigger messages, it needs a registry, named owner, permission scope, sandbox, network limits, prompt and action logging, human approvals for external actions, cost controls, incident response, and a kill switch.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that these are internal evaluations and preliminary thresholds, not a public model doing this in production.

HOST: Right. But preliminary is the point. Capability warnings are most useful before the tool is everywhere. On the product side, OpenAI also updated GPT-5.6 Sol in ChatGPT for Plus and Pro users, expanded GPT-5.6 Luna access for free users, and added a Think button for harder questions. In the API, Fast mode now supports long-context prompts above 272,000 tokens for GPT-5.6 Sol, Terra, and Luna, with speeds up to 2.5 times faster than Standard. That affects real workflows: policy review, codebase analysis, loan-file review, due diligence, long transcripts, and customer-support audits.

HOST: The useful takeaway is model routing. Use stronger models where judgment, code, cyber, or complex documents matter. Use cheaper models for high-volume routine tasks. Keep evaluations private and workflow-specific. And do not mistake faster long-context processing for permission to dump sensitive data into a weak process.

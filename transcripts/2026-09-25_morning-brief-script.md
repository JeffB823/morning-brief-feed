CHAPTER: Columbus
HOST: In Columbus, the local operating story is now on the city calendar. File 2560-2026 is on the September 28 council agenda, and it would require Columbus Water and Power to establish peak rates, fees or charges for large-load, high-volume industrial and manufacturing users.
COHOST: Is this still mostly a data-center story?
HOST: Data centers are the pressure point, but the ordinance is broader. The city file says Central Ohio has more than one hundred operational data centers and close to two hundred statewide, while chip fabrication, breweries, beverage makers and food processors can also strain water, sewer and power systems.
COHOST: What would actually change if council passes it?
HOST: The department would have to bring back a concrete rate proposal instead of relying only on ordinary volumetric charges. The options named in the file include tiered or block water charges, demand charges based on meter size or peak capacity, and minimum bills tied to contracted capacity.
COHOST: Why should a household care about an industrial rate design?
HOST: Because the city says a volumetric-only structure can shift infrastructure costs onto residential and small-commercial customers whose demand is flatter. The Home Road Water Plant adds forty-eight million gallons a day of capacity, and the policy question is who pays for capacity built to handle peak industrial load.
COHOST: The watch item is not whether leaders like or dislike data centers. It is whether the proposed rate actually prices peak demand.
HOST: That is the useful test. Monday's vote would not write the final bill, but it would tell Columbus Water and Power to produce one.
COHOST: And it gives residents a clearer place to look. Instead of arguing about growth in the abstract, they can ask for the demand assumption, the rate class, the capacity charge and how small users are protected.
CHAPTER: Home Lending

COHOST: Which loan file feels that first?
HOST: Investor and owner-occupied files using rental income to qualify. The workflow is not only reading a cleaner guide. Underwriters, processors and quality-control teams have to update training, bookmarked sections, income worksheets and exception handling before the mandatory date.
COHOST: Did Fannie Mae expand who qualifies, or is this mostly documentation?
HOST: The source says the section was rewritten for clarity, navigation and usability, with some policy updates and clarifications. So the safe operating posture is to treat it as an implementation project: compare old and new rental-income steps, identify any changed treatment, and make sure loan-origination system aids point to the right guide text.
COHOST: Why does that matter to a borrower?
HOST: A borrower using lease income, departure-residence income or investment-property rent can lose time if a file is underwritten from stale instructions. For loan officers, the consequence is better upfront document collection and fewer last-minute conditions. For lenders, the consequence is rep-and-warrant risk if a November file is still reviewed under the old map.
COHOST: What is the next thing to watch?
HOST: Whether Fannie publishes more FAQs before November 1, and whether aggregators or investors issue overlays that make the transition stricter than the base guide.
COHOST: That is especially important for mixed-use borrowers and small landlords. If an investor overlay asks for more history or cleaner leases, the borrower may not find out until the file is already moving.

CHAPTER: Frontier AI
HOST: On the frontier, the fresh item is a privacy benchmark rather than another launch. Prosaic Intelligence published ConfAIde data today, scoring fourteen API model configurations on whether they reveal private information when social norms say it should stay confidential.
COHOST: What bar does that clear?
HOST: Benchmark and safety evaluation. The headline metric is privacy-sharing error rate, converted to a zero-to-one-hundred score. Claude Opus 5.5 with medium reasoning and Zhipu's GLM 5.3 Flash with high reasoning both scored 98.62, while GPT-6 Sol with medium reasoning scored 98.37.
COHOST: Those scores are close. Should buyers treat that as a ranking?
HOST: Carefully. The page says the headline tier uses 270 single-turn scenarios, ten repeats, rule-based grading and API model configurations. It also says measurement dates, sample sizes and confidence intervals are not included in the score-only feed, and the results do not establish how a consumer app behaves with its own prompts, tools or safeguards.
COHOST: So the useful result is less "which model won" and more "what kind of privacy mistake is being tested."
HOST: Yes. The example task asks whether a model lists people who should not know a confidential fact. That maps directly to enterprise risk: customer notes, HR issues, investigation files and relationship-manager context can all leak through an assistant that over-shares. For a regulated bank, the earliest use is vendor screening for privacy-sensitive copilots, gated by internal test sets that match the bank's own data and permission model.
COHOST: The missing confidence intervals matter too. When scores are separated by a few tenths of a point, a procurement team should not pretend it has a hard ordering unless the underlying measurement supports that precision.

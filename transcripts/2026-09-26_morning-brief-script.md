CHAPTER: National
HOST: Start nationally with the Supreme Court's Friday emergency order in Department of Homeland Security versus League of Women Voters. The Court stayed a lower-court order and let the government use the modified SAVE system while the case continues.
COHOST: What does that actually let states do before the election?
HOST: It lets federal, state and local officials use the Department of Homeland Security database for voter-eligibility checks for now. Cornell's published order notes SAVE has long been used to verify whether people are citizens, but the dispute is over the Trump administration's expanded version and the data-sharing behind it.
COHOST: Does this mean states can immediately conduct a mass voter purge?
HOST: Not cleanly. The order itself points to the National Voter Registration Act's 90-day limit on systematic list maintenance before a federal election. The practical effect is more likely individualized checks, challenges and election-office workload, unless a state tests the boundary and gets sued.
HOST: The reason it matters is operational. Election officials, voting-rights groups and county clerks now have a live database pathway to monitor just weeks before voting. The government's argument is election integrity. The dissenting concern is privacy and the risk that eligible voters get flagged too late to fix the record.
COHOST: So the useful watch is not the slogan around noncitizen voting. It is whether a state uses SAVE results to challenge a real voter record, and whether the voter gets notice and a fast correction path.
COHOST: There is also a county-capacity issue. A database match is not self-executing; someone has to interpret it, contact the voter, preserve the record and explain what happens if the person shows up during early voting.

CHAPTER: Home Lending
HOST: In home lending, the operational change is Desktop Underwriter behavior this weekend. Fannie Mae's DU version 12.1 September update applies to loan casefiles submitted or resubmitted after the evening of September 25.
COHOST: Which file does that change first?
HOST: Any conventional file where DU findings drive underwriting conditions, fraud review, collateral review or income validation. The update changes the Potential Red Flag section, adds occupancy and subject-property messages, and uses Collateral Underwriter information for new eligibility messages.
COHOST: Give me the practical underwriter version.
HOST: If an appraisal is tied to an appraiser on Fannie Mae's No Longer Accepted list, DU can issue an Ineligible recommendation and require a new appraisal. If the property condition is C6, DU also issues Ineligible. And if CU flags heightened overvaluation risk, the lender gets a message to review whether value is supported.
HOST: Income validation also changes. For fixed base income, DU moves from requiring 12 months of current employment to at least three months of current employment plus 12 months of total employment history. Variable base income still needs 12 months of current employment.
COHOST: That sounds like eligibility, integrations and training all at once.
HOST: It is. Fannie also says subject rental income on a one-unit principal residence or second home will now make a casefile Ineligible, except for accessory dwelling unit income on a one-unit principal residence. Integrations parsing DU messages may need updates because the impact memo lists new, modified and retired messages.
COHOST: So the borrower may hear only that a condition changed, but the lender has to know whether DU changed the file, the data field, the appraisal path or the income validation clock.
HOST: Correct. The watch is Monday pipeline cleanup: how many resubmitted files throw new red flags, ineligible findings or integration defects after the weekend release.
COHOST: The risk is highest for teams that treat automated underwriting like a static black box. When the findings report changes, processors, underwriters, appraisers and technology vendors all need the same map of what the message means.
CHAPTER: AI in Banking
HOST: For AI in Banking, I added LoanPro to the deployment ledger. The lane is consumer servicing and collections, and the evidence rank is company claim carried in a published launch article.
COHOST: What changed in the row?
HOST: LoanPro announced LoanPro MCP, part of its Intelligence Suite, with phase one live now. It lets lenders connect AI agents and models to LoanPro for pulling account context and history, processing payments, setting up autopay and recording promises to pay.
COHOST: That is more concrete than a generic AI assistant. What is the old workflow?
HOST: A human servicing agent hunts through account history, payment status, communications, compliance flags and next-step procedures before acting. LoanPro says its AI Account Briefing summarizes health indicators, recent activity and suggested next steps, while MCP lets the agent operate through existing controls.
HOST: The disclosed metric is vendor-reported: LoanPro says agents spent an average of 20 percent of each servicing call looking for information, and its testing reduced that to 10 to 15 percent, and to 5 percent in some cases. There is no independent result for error rate, borrower outcome, complaint movement or regulatory finding.
COHOST: What would this change at a large bank or lender?
HOST: It changes control design for servicing automation. If an AI agent can set up autopay or record a promise to pay, the institution has to prove the agent had the same authority as the employee using it and that the audit trail records the responsible person.
COHOST: The key limit is attribution, then.
HOST: Yes. LoanPro says every request runs through role-based access controls and compliance guardrails, and each action is recorded under that person's name. The next proof point is customer evidence showing volume, exception rate, corrections and whether borrower calls actually improve outside the vendor test.
COHOST: That is where servicing differs from a harmless internal summary. A promise to pay, autopay setup or payment action can become a complaint, collection dispute or exam question if the borrower later says the account history is wrong.

Columbus

Host: In Columbus, the fresh local operating story is data-center utility cost. Axios Columbus reports Council President Shannon Hardin and Councilmember Christopher Wyche are backing new guardrails for future data center projects, and the first ordinance is already on the September 28 council agenda.

Co-host: What would that ordinance actually do?

Host: The city file says it would require Columbus Water and Power to establish peak rates or other fees for large load and high-volume industrial and manufacturing users, including data centers. The point is cost causation: big, variable users should pay for infrastructure they force the system to build.

Co-host: That sounds broader than an anti-data-center measure.

Host: It is written more broadly. The file mentions chip fabrication, brewing and food processing too. But data-center growth is the pressure point, and the legislation says Central Ohio has more than one hundred operational data centers, with close to two hundred statewide.

Co-host: Why does a water-and-power rate design matter to a resident who never sees the server building?

Host: Because the question is who pays for capacity. If a new 48-million-gallon-per-day Home Road Water Plant or power infrastructure supports peak industrial demand, residents and small businesses do not want the cost folded quietly into ordinary bills. The package also points toward renewable-energy requirements, water-use disclosure, reclaimed-water encouragement and decommissioning plans.

Co-host: The tradeoff is that Central Ohio wants the jobs and tax base, but residents want proof the upside is not subsidized through utility bills.

Host: Exactly. Watch the September 28 vote and whether Columbus Water and Power comes back with a concrete rate design rather than a slogan.

Co-host: The useful test is transparency. If council wants compromise instead of backlash, residents need to see the demand charge, capacity assumption and environmental conditions before the next project asks for approvals.

AI in Banking

Host: For AI in Banking, I changed the JPMorganChase ledger row. Business Insider reports the bank is putting some Claude use behind a 2,000-dollar monthly spending limit and a broader security architecture called Devspace.

Co-host: What changed in the row: usage, cost control, or security?

Host: Security and internal-productivity controls. The task lane is software engineering and internal productivity. Developers had piloted Claude directly on desktops; Devspace is described as a containerized, sandbox-like server in Amazon Web Services that restricts Claude's access to employee credentials and internal systems.

Co-host: What is the evidence rank?

Host: Reputable reporting, with named comments from JPMorgan's security leadership. The useful numbers are scope, not outcome metrics: Business Insider says an internal dashboard showed about 8,000 Claude licenses in a 65,000-person technology division, and a Devspace Teams group had about 1,900 members in early September. There is no disclosed metric for code quality, defect reduction or developer hours saved.

Co-host: What would this change at a large bank?

Host: It changes the approval path for coding assistants. Instead of asking only whether Claude makes engineers faster, a bank asks whether the assistant has its own identity, no standing entitlements and no direct path to customer data, credentials or internal systems.

Co-host: The limitation is that a sandbox does not prove the code is good.

Host: Exactly. It is a containment control, not a productivity study. The next upgrade would be a bank-disclosed comparison of speed, defects, security findings and review time after engineers move into the sandboxed workflow.

Frontier AI

Host: On the frontier, Anthropic disclosed a self-improvement metric that is more concrete than another warning speech. AP reports Claude is now leading 26 percent of Anthropic's model research and development, meaning it can complete most of a task end to end from a high-level prompt while still under human supervision.

Co-host: What bar does that clear for the frontier segment?

Host: Agent and tool-use advances, plus a safety measurement. Anthropic says about 90 percent of its research and development is now done in collaboration with Claude, and AP reports roughly 30,000 agents were doing research and engineering work as of August.

Co-host: Is that recursive self-improvement already?

Host: Anthropic says no. The model is not working completely autonomously, and AP says it is unclear how close the company thinks it is to that threshold. The measured part is the disclosed share of model R&D led or assisted by Claude. The claimed lesson is that labs should publish comparable methods so outsiders can judge whether AI is accelerating AI development.

Co-host: Who is advantaged if this keeps moving?

Host: Frontier labs with strong internal agent platforms and oversight systems. The risk is that progress becomes harder for outsiders to measure because the tools building the next model are partly hidden inside the lab. For a regulated bank, the earliest use is not self-improving AI; it is asking vendors for comparable agent-oversight metrics before approving autonomous development tools on sensitive code.

Co-host: That bridges back to JPMorgan nicely. The lab is measuring how much work agents can lead. The bank is asking how little authority an agent can have while still helping engineers.

Host: That distinction matters. Capability growth is exciting at the lab. Inside a regulated firm, the safer milestone is useful work with bounded authority, monitored actions and a human still accountable for the decision.

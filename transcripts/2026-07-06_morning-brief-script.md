HOST: Good morning. It is Monday, July 6th, 2026, and this is your Morning Brief.

COHOST: The useful frame today is what has to restart after a long holiday weekend: diplomacy, bond trading, school accountability, local election administration, and A I systems that are powerful enough to require real controls.

HOST: Start with the national and global story, because it is also a markets story and a security story. In Tehran this morning, mourners filled the streets for the funeral procession of Iran’s late Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei. The Associated Press reports that his coffin, along with coffins for family members killed with him in the February 28th airstrike that opened the war launched by Israel and the United States, moved through a massive crowd toward Mehrabad International Airport.

COHOST: The fresh development is not only the funeral. It is that the funeral has become a show of regime strength, public grief, and anti-U.S. anger at the exact moment negotiations are supposed to resume.

HOST: Exactly. Iranian state television showed crowds stretching for miles, and A P reported that mourners carried signs calling for President Trump’s death. Officials used misting water and loudspeakers to manage heat and crowd pressure. Streets, airspace, and normal activity have been shut down, with mourning events expected to continue until Khamenei is buried Thursday at the Imam Reza shrine in Mashhad.

HOST: The practical consequence is that diplomacy now has to move through a highly charged domestic-political moment. The U.S. and Iran have been negotiating over a permanent end to the war, the Strait of Hormuz, and nuclear and regional issues. But the Iranian leadership has to show defiance at home, while the Trump administration has to manage security threats, oil-market risk, and the politics of having helped launch the strike that killed Iran’s former leader.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that state funerals are choreographed. A huge crowd does not tell us exactly how much room Iran’s new leadership has to compromise.

HOST: Right. The watch item is whether funeral-week rhetoric turns into operational escalation: threats against U.S. officials, militia activity, shipping disruptions, cyber activity, or a breakdown in talks. If the Strait of Hormuz remains open and negotiations restart after the burial, markets can treat this as contained risk. If shipping or retaliation threats intensify, oil, inflation expectations, and Treasury yields all become part of the story.

COHOST: The unusually important detail is timing. Thursday’s burial is not just ceremonial. It is the earliest clean point when leaders on both sides can test whether the pause was a face-saving interval or the start of a longer freeze.

HOST: And that is why energy desks, national security teams, shipping insurers, and companies with Middle East exposure will watch signals that are smaller than a formal announcement. A military alert, a cyber advisory, a tanker rerouting decision, or a hard-line speech from Iran’s new leadership could matter before diplomats sit back down. The interpretation weakens if the crowd fades without follow-through and negotiators quietly resume contact. It strengthens if the funeral becomes the mobilizing event for retaliation.

HOST: Bring that discipline home to Central Ohio. The freshest Columbus story is education accountability. W O S U reports that the Ohio Department of Education and Workforce is investigating Columbus City Schools after the district self-reported testing anomalies at Independence and Walnut Ridge high schools. The district says it found irregularities during an internal review of spring state tests.

COHOST: This matters beyond two buildings because test scores affect students, families, school ratings, public trust, and district management at a time when Columbus schools are already under budget pressure.

HOST: That is the practical point. Columbus City Schools says it will invalidate spring scores for all state tests in all subjects at those two schools. Students will be allowed to retake exams over the summer or next school year. The state also sent information to the State Board of Education’s Office of Professional Conduct, and the district says it will increase monitoring and staff training.

HOST: The affected parties are students whose scores may not count, teachers and administrators who may face scrutiny, families trying to understand graduation or course-placement implications, and local taxpayers who want confidence that reported outcomes are real. The counter-signal is that the district self-reported the issue, which is usually better than waiting for an external discovery. But self-reporting does not answer the operational questions: what happened, who knew, how widespread it was, and whether retesting creates real disruption for students.

COHOST: The next watch item is concrete. Watch for the state’s investigation timeline, whether professional-conduct findings are opened, and whether Columbus school board members demand a public explanation before the next school year.

HOST: The other local civic watch is Whitehall. Tomorrow, July 7th, is the automatic recount in the recall election. Certified provisional ballots flipped Council Member Lori Elmore from an apparent six-vote loss to an eight-vote lead, while Mayor Michael Bivens and Council Member Amy Harcar remain ahead. The reason to keep this alive is simple: the next event is tomorrow, and it can decide whether the city moves forward or reopens a legitimacy fight over a very narrow result.

COHOST: For Whitehall residents, the issue is not only who sits in office. It is whether the city can get back to work on development, policing tension, fiscal trust, and public engagement after a bruising recall.

HOST: On home lending and mortgage, Monday is the rate-window test. Freddie Mac’s latest survey put the 30-year fixed mortgage at 6.43 percent on July 2nd, down from 6.49 percent a week earlier. The 15-year fixed was 5.79 percent. The next Freddie Mac release is Thursday, and the next M B A applications report is Wednesday.

COHOST: The useful takeaway is that lower rates are necessary, but they are not sufficient. A small rate improvement does not automatically fix affordability.

HOST: Correct. Borrowers are still dealing with price, taxes, insurance, credit profile, down payment, cash to close, and job confidence. For loan officers, the operational move is borrower triage: refresh payment scenarios for people who were close to qualifying, contact shoppers with realistic lock thresholds, and avoid treating every inquiry as a live loan. For lenders, the real metric is pull-through. If pricing gets people to call but not lock, the business has more activity without more revenue.

HOST: The counter-signal is that last week’s better rate survey was taken before the market had a full post-holiday read on jobs, oil, and Treasury supply. Early Monday trading showed Treasury yields edging lower, which helps the mortgage story, but the interpretation can weaken quickly if oil risk from Iran rises or if Fed minutes later this week push investors back toward higher-rate expectations.

COHOST: So the watch item for mortgage teams is Wednesday’s application data and Thursday’s rate survey, but the day-to-day operating signal is still the 10-year Treasury and mortgage-backed-securities pricing.

HOST: The borrower-facing message should be narrow and honest. A 6.43 percent survey rate is useful for payment math, but it is not the quote every borrower will see, and it does not remove points, fees, credit adjustments, or property-level costs. The better lender conversation is scenario-based: here is the payment if rates hold, here is the lock trigger if pricing improves, and here is the document or debt issue that still blocks approval. That makes a rate window operational instead of promotional.

HOST: The A I angle for mortgage companies is governance under production pressure. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have both been pushing clearer expectations around A I and machine-learning controls. The practical message for lenders is that A I can help with document review, borrower follow-up, servicing explanations, fraud detection, quality control, and staff productivity, but only if the tool has access limits, audit trails, human review, vendor oversight, and a measurable business outcome.

COHOST: In other words, the weak version is speed without evidence. The strong version is faster work that can still survive compliance review.

HOST: In broader A I and technology, the latest-model story is busy but the through-line is clear. OpenAI previewed GPT-5.6 Sol, with Terra and Luna as lower-cost and faster options in the same family. OpenAI says Sol is its strongest model yet, with improved agentic coding, biology, and cybersecurity performance, plus a new max reasoning level and an ultra mode that uses subagents for complex work. But access is limited first to trusted partners, with broader release planned later.

COHOST: That matters for enterprises because the frontier is becoming both more capable and more gated. You cannot build a critical workflow assuming every model will be broadly available on your timeline.

HOST: Anthropic is sending the same signal from a different direction. Claude Sonnet 5 is now broadly available across plans, with introductory A P I pricing through August 31st. It is designed to bring agentic work, coding, and professional tasks closer to everyday enterprise use. At the same time, Anthropic restored Fable 5 globally after U.S. export controls were lifted, while Mythos remains the more sensitive cyber-capability reference point. Anthropic’s own explanation is that high-capability models need defense in depth, misuse monitoring, and careful access policy.

HOST: Google adds the distribution story. Gemini 3.5 Flash is now the default model in Google’s A I Mode for Search, and Google is pushing information agents, agentic booking, business calling, and generative user interfaces into mainstream search workflows. That means agent behavior is moving out of developer demos and into consumer and enterprise surfaces people already use.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that not every agent release becomes reliable production work. Some will be too expensive, too brittle, too hard to audit, or too risky around permissions.

HOST: Exactly. The best-practice takeaway for banks, mortgage companies, and large enterprises is model-agnostic infrastructure. Keep a model inventory. Classify tasks by risk. Run private evaluations on real workflows. Limit data access. Require human approval for sensitive actions. Log what the agent saw and did. Set cost caps. Maintain fallback models. And give owners a kill switch. The winner is not just the company with the smartest model. It is the company that turns model capability into governed, measurable workflow improvement.

COHOST: The implementation test is whether the organization learns from each run. If agents produce drafts, decisions, or actions, the feedback from experts has to improve the workflow, not disappear into chat history.

HOST: That is the enterprise A I maturity gap. Sophisticated teams are building evaluation sets from their own work, capturing where humans corrected the model, routing low-risk work to cheaper models, and keeping sensitive tasks behind approvals. That is how A I moves from pilot theater to production infrastructure. For regulated firms, the most valuable question is not, can the model answer? It is, can we prove why we trusted it, where we constrained it, and when a human overruled it?

Watch whether the S and P 500 advance is broad, whether the Nasdaq recovers from last week’s pressure, and whether financials, industrials, and health care keep participating. For borrowers, the most practical number remains the 10-year Treasury. For investors, the practical question is whether the rally is broadening or still leaning too heavily on crowded A I winners.

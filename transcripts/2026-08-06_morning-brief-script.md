Her confirmation became a test of whether she would defend science-based guidance if Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Junior pushed another direction.

The practical consequence is decision speed. A weak C D C leaves local officials guessing during outbreaks, storms, heat events, and vaccine planning.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that one confirmation does not rebuild a workforce or settle the Kennedy question.

HOST: Right. Watch when Schwartz starts, who fills other health-agency vacancies, whether C D C guidance changes in vaccine or outbreak policy, and whether career staff see protections strong enough to stop more departures.

HOST: The second national story is legal capacity for migrant children. AP reports the Trump administration awarded a 150 million dollar, year-long contract to the Houston-based Burke Law Group to provide legal services for unaccompanied migrant children in government shelters. The contract starts August 15th and was awarded as a single-source agreement, meaning it bypassed standard competitive bidding.

COHOST: The fresh issue is not only who got paid. It is whether children in deportation proceedings get meaningful representation during a fast-moving enforcement push.

HOST: Exactly. Legal-aid groups that previously handled the work say the new firm has little immigration-law depth and political ties to the administration. AP found only two of 25 lawyers listed on the firm's website claimed immigration-law experience. The contract covers roughly 1,800 children in shelters, while about 22,000 children live outside shelters with sponsors and may need separate coverage.

HOST: The affected parties are unaccompanied children, sponsors, immigration courts, shelter operators, legal-aid providers, H H S, and receiving communities. The practical consequence is due process. If representation is disrupted, hearings can be delayed, private information can move through unclear channels, and children may face deportation proceedings without lawyers who understand asylum, trafficking protections, or family reunification.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that the government may argue it had to fill a gap quickly after the prior contract ended.

HOST: That is possible. But watch whether H H S explains the sole-source decision, whether courts see confusion from substitute attorneys, whether the outside-shelter population gets a contract, and whether prior providers are paid without breaching client confidentiality. If the handoff fails, the legal right exists on paper but not in the courtroom.

HOST: The global story is the Strait of Hormuz. Iran said Wednesday it is in the final stage of drafting an agreement with Oman, and President Trump says a deal could be announced this week. AP reports Iranian and Omani negotiators have finalized a draft and are waiting on approval from Iran's supreme leader.

COHOST: This is why markets keep reacting to diplomacy. Hormuz is not just a map point. It is the pressure valve for oil, gas, insurance, shipping, and inflation expectations.

HOST: Before the war, about a fifth of the world's traded oil and natural gas moved through the strait. The fresh question is what both sides have to concede. Iran wants some measure of control and does not want to return to the old open-waterway arrangement. The U.S. has said it does not want a deal that locks in Iranian control over a global shipping lane. The deal may also depend on the U.S. lifting its blockade on Iranian ports.

HOST: The affected parties are refiners, airlines, trucking companies, importers, manufacturers, consumers, central banks, mortgage borrowers, and investors. The practical consequence is inflation pressure. If ships move safely and oil stays below 80 dollars Brent, long yields can ease. If the deal grants Iran control that shippers or insurers do not trust, the headline will not lower real costs for long.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that final-stage drafting is not final approval, and reopening a route requires operational proof.

HOST: Exactly. Watch the text of any Oman-Iran statement, U.S. reaction to port-blockade language, tanker traffic, insurance pricing, Brent and W T I levels, and whether Friday's jobs data changes rate expectations more than oil does.

HOST: In home lending and mortgage, the fresh datapoint is M B A's weekly survey. Mortgage applications fell 2.9 percent for the week ending July 31st. Purchase applications fell 4 percent, refinance applications fell 2 percent, and M B A's chief economist said both channels are now running behind last year's pace.

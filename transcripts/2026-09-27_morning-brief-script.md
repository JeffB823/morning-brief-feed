CHAPTER: National
HOST: Start nationally with Boeing. The fresh issue is not another broad argument about the Max. CBS News reported Saturday night that the FAA is investigating a software glitch in some 737 Max jets involving the flight-management computer during an aborted landing.
COHOST: Is this about planes already flying, or about deliveries that have not happened yet?
HOST: Mostly deliveries and certification risk for now. Alaska, American, Southwest and United told CBS their in-service Max fleets do not use the software version at issue. But Southwest and United have told Boeing they will not accept new aircraft with that operating system.
COHOST: What does the glitch actually change for a crew?
HOST: In a rare missed-approach scenario, with autopilot engaged and the flight plan changed, vertical navigation could disengage and fall back to a less sophisticated pitch-control mode. Boeing says pilots are trained to land without VNAV, but the concern is workload at low altitude when crews are already busy.
COHOST: So the public-policy part is the FAA review board, not immediate grounding.
HOST: Right. The FAA says it will convene a Corrective Action Review Board and act if it identifies a safety concern. The business consequence is timing: the issue could complicate 737 Max 10 certification and Max 7 deliveries just as Boeing needs smoother handoffs to repair cash flow and airline trust.
COHOST: The useful watch is whether airlines keep refusing that software version, because that tells us whether operators trust Boeing's temporary guidance while the permanent fix is still being accelerated.
COHOST: It also shows how software confidence has become part of aircraft inventory planning. An airline can believe pilots can handle the procedure and still decide that accepting a new jet with a known software issue creates too much operational and reputational risk.

COHOST: So the action item is not "rates moved."
HOST: Correct. The near-term watch is Monday production monitoring: lenders and technology service providers should compare government-loan test cases, integration impact notes and live findings so a message change does not masquerade as borrower ineligibility.
COHOST: That is the difference between an operations desk and a sales desk. A loan officer may only need to know whether the borrower can proceed, but the operations team has to know whether the answer changed because the borrower changed, the agency rule changed, or the software translation changed.
CHAPTER: AI in Banking
HOST: For the banking AI segment, I moved DBS out of the open-research bucket and into the deployment ledger as an announced operating-economics item. The lane is software engineering and internal productivity, and the evidence rank is trade-reporting summary, not an audited result.
COHOST: What changed about the row?
HOST: AISeng's September 25 fintech brief, summarizing The Edge Singapore, says DBS is matching individual banking tasks to the most cost-efficient model that can complete them safely. Routine work can use smaller internally hosted open-source models, while repeated work can use caching to cut processing cost.
COHOST: That sounds less dramatic than an agent paying a bill. Why cover it?
HOST: Because cost control is what moves AI from demo to budget line. A large bank cannot run every routine task on the priciest frontier model and call that scale. The decision changes architecture: which tasks need frontier reasoning, which can use a smaller model, and where governance connects them.
COHOST: Is there a disclosed metric?
HOST: None in the available reporting. That is the limitation. We have a named bank and a concrete operating method, but no task volume, defect rate, latency, cost-per-case or business outcome. The useful evidence would be a before-and-after on a real workflow, not a quote about efficiency.
COHOST: What would this change at a large bank?
HOST: It changes model-selection governance. Procurement, risk and engineering teams need a repeatable way to approve the cheapest model that is good enough for a task, while keeping higher-risk work on stronger models with stricter review.
COHOST: It also changes vendor negotiation. If a bank can prove that routine summarization, classification or code-support work runs safely on smaller internal models, it has more leverage when a vendor prices every workflow as though it needs the flagship model.

COHOST: That boundary is what keeps this from being a generic "AI for cyber" story. A defender needs speed, but the closer a model gets to exploit validation or remediation, the more important it becomes to prove who authorized the test and what system was in scope.
HOST: For a regulated bank, the earliest plausible use is sanctioned cyber testing through an approved provider or internal security team, gated by legal authorization, change control and evidence that model output was reviewed before remediation.

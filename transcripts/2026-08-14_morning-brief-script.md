HOST: Start with the national economic story. Yesterday's P P I report gave markets the confirmation they wanted, but not the all-clear they may be tempted to hear. The Bureau of Labor Statistics said producer prices for final demand were unchanged in July, after falling 0.1 percent in June and rising 0.5 percent in May. Goods prices fell 0.7 percent, helped by lower energy and gasoline. Services prices rose 0.2 percent. Construction prices rose 2.2 percent.

COHOST: So the headline is calm. The details are mixed.

HOST: Exactly. The fresh hook is that cooler C P I was followed by flat wholesale inflation. That strengthens the case that July price pressure was contained enough for the Fed to wait at the September meeting. But the counter-signal is underneath the surface: final demand excluding food, energy, and trade services rose 0.4 percent in July, and that measure is up 4.7 percent from a year earlier. That says some service and margin pressure is still alive.

HOST: The affected parties are households, retailers, manufacturers, logistics firms, lenders, bond investors, and Fed officials. The practical consequence is margin planning. If goods and energy cool while services and construction stay firm, businesses do not all experience the same inflation story. A retailer may get some freight or gasoline relief. A builder may not. A lender may see Treasury relief before borrowers see meaningful payment relief.

COHOST: The next test is retail sales at 8:30 Eastern.

HOST: Right. The Census Bureau is scheduled to release July retail sales this morning. The CNBC and N R F Retail Monitor already showed July core retail sales up 0.3 percent from June and 4.72 percent from a year ago, but that measure uses card data and excludes autos and gas. The government report will tell us whether consumers are still spending broadly, or whether the apparent resilience is concentrated. A soft retail number plus calm C P I and P P I would support the Fed-pause argument. A firm number would say demand is still strong enough to keep inflation risk alive.

AP reports two tankers operated by Abu Dhabi's state-owned ADNOC were attacked by drones Thursday evening while transiting the Strait of Hormuz. The company said there were no injuries and the situation was controlled. The U A E blamed Iran and called the attacks piracy. Iran had not immediately responded.

The Wall Street Journal reported Brent near 88 dollars this morning after the attack, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said new financial measures against Iran are coming next week. That puts households, airlines, truckers, manufacturers, refiners, and central bankers back in the same chain of exposure.

HOST: The counter-signal is that the reported damage was minor, and oil is still below the 90-dollar level that has been the clean psychological marker this week. But the watch item is concrete: whether insurers raise war-risk costs, whether traffic through Hormuz slows further, whether the U.S. sanctions package has any immediate bite left, and whether gasoline prices start to undo the inflation relief.

Franklin County has 158 reported cases, up more than 20 from last week, and Licking County has 41. Cyclosporiasis is an intestinal infection tied to a parasite, and the Ohio Department of Health is telling people to wash or fully cook vegetables before eating them.

HOST: The consequence is practical, especially after storms and during late-summer produce season. Restaurants, grocers, farmers markets, schools, households, and health departments should treat produce handling as a live operational issue, not a background advisory. The uncertainty is source tracing. A case count can grow before investigators clearly identify specific supply chains or exposure points. Watch county-level updates, any produce-specific advisory, and whether Franklin County's increase accelerates next week.

HOST: In home lending and mortgage, Freddie Mac's Thursday update gave the market a small break. The 30-year fixed-rate mortgage averaged 6.67 percent as of August 13th, down from 6.69 percent the week before. The 15-year averaged 5.96 percent, down from 6.01 percent.

COHOST: A two-basis-point move does not fix affordability. But it does tell us borrowers are rate-sensitive.

HOST: Exactly. Freddie Mac's own commentary said affordability has improved from a year ago and that recent increases in purchase and refinance applications suggest borrowers respond even to modest changes. The affected parties are purchase borrowers, first-time buyers, move-up sellers, builders, real estate agents, loan officers, lock desks, servicers, and secondary-market teams.

HOST: The practical consequence is not to celebrate 6.67 percent. It is to segment the pipeline. Borrowers who are barely outside approval may need a tighter payment quote, a seller credit, a buydown, or a different property target. Borrowers with strong credit and equity may be worth revisiting for home-equity or cash-out conversations if their household need is real. Builders need to know whether a slight rate dip changes qualified traffic or just adds more shoppers who still cannot clear monthly-payment math.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury, but not perfectly.

HOST: Correct. Mortgage-backed security spreads, lender capacity, servicing values, borrower pull-through, and geopolitical oil risk all matter. If retail sales are firm or Hormuz drives yields higher, today's relief can fade quickly. Watch next week's M B A applications, the 10-year Treasury near the mid-4s, and whether lenders improve rate sheets enough to change real borrower decisions.

HOST: The home-lending A I implementation story is still governance, but today's broader A I price news changes the business case. If model prices are falling and agent performance is improving, lenders will be tempted to expand document review, borrower communication, quality control, servicing chat, fraud triage, pricing analytics, and staff copilots.

COHOST: That makes Fannie Mae's governance framework more important, not less.

HOST: Exactly. Lower model cost can make more workflows economical. It does not remove model inventory, vendor oversight, data-permission limits, information security, audit logs, monitoring, human review, and clear ownership. For regulated lenders, the real question is where A I touches the system of record or borrower-facing communication. The practical move is to approve use cases by workflow, not by brand. A cheaper model can be a good back-office analyst and still be wrong for an unsupervised credit or servicing action.

COHOST: The point is not that every enterprise should switch. The point is that cost-per-task is now a board-level metric.

That matters for banks, mortgage firms, service desks, compliance teams, and software organizations because the spend is no longer just token price. It is task completion, number of turns, human rework, latency, auditability, and failure rate.

HOST: The counter-signal is that public benchmarks are not private proof. A model that looks efficient in a leaderboard can still fail on a lender's documents, policy exceptions, customer tone, or regulated disclosures. The next watch item is whether enterprises build model routing: fast models for front-line response, stronger models for complex review, cheaper long-running models for batch research or quality control, and human approval where the workflow carries legal or customer risk.

Scharf's job is inside the White House: advising on executive orders, pardons, legislation, litigation strategy, oversight, privilege claims, and the legal risks around presidential decisions.

Scharf, meanwhile, is tied to the White House operating system and to the planning commission that has been central to Trump's 400 million dollar ballroom project. A federal appeals court has halted aboveground ballroom construction unless Congress authorizes it, while allowing some underground security work to continue during the appeal window.

COHOST: So the affected parties are not just lawyers. They are agencies, Congress, federal contractors, prosecutors, courts, watchdogs, defendants, donors, and anyone dealing with federal enforcement or oversight.

A confirmed attorney general can move faster on department priorities. Together, they give the administration more discipline and more durability. The counter-signal is that more legal talent does not eliminate outside checks. Courts can still block actions. Inspectors general can still investigate. Congress can still demand records, especially if the House changes hands in the midterms. And private parties can still sue.

For Blanche, watch first personnel moves, written instructions on politically sensitive investigations, and whether judges start asking sharper questions about Justice Department motives.

HOST: In Central Ohio, today's Columbus City Council hearing on Flock license-plate cameras has become a live governance test. The hearing is set for this afternoon after a Columbus police audit raised questions about more than 15,000 possible immigration-related searches in the city's Flock system. Mayor Andrew Ginther has already ordered police to stop statewide sharing. Then W O S U reported that two former Reynoldsburg officers may have made as many as 100 personal searches using that department's license-plate readers.

COHOST: That changes the hearing from a general privacy debate into a control-design question.

HOST: Right. License-plate readers can help find stolen vehicles, suspects, and missing people. Police chiefs will make that case. But the risk is that the same database can become a searchable movement log for ordinary drivers, immigrants, domestic-violence survivors, journalists, political organizers, or anyone else whose plate can be queried without a tight purpose rule.

HOST: The affected parties are drivers, crime victims, immigrants, police officers, prosecutors, defense lawyers, suburbs connected to shared databases, and city officials responsible for the contract. The practical consequence is policy language. If Columbus keeps the cameras, the useful questions are not abstract. Which crimes qualify? Who may search? What explanation must be entered? How long is data retained? Which outside agencies can access it? Are immigration searches barred or limited? How often are random audits run? Who reviews sensitive searches? What is published to the public? And what discipline follows misuse?

COHOST: The counter-signal is important: Reynoldsburg found the alleged misuse through an internal investigation. Logs are not useless if audits are regular, independent, and tied to consequences.

HOST: Exactly. The weak version of today's hearing is a long list of concerns followed by no binding change. The stronger version is a contract and policy reset: narrow use, short retention, supervisor approval for sensitive categories, documented purpose, outside-sharing limits, random audits, public reporting, and cancellation rights if the vendor or an outside agency violates local rules.

HOST: A second Central Ohio watch item is Wednesday's immigration appointment point. W O S U reviewed a Department of Homeland Security letter telling at least one area Haitian who had Temporary Protected Status to report to the I C E office on Brooksedge Boulevard in Westerville on August 12th. ABC News has also reported that hundreds of Haitian immigrants in Ohio have been summoned for I C E check-ins, and advocates say some have received ankle monitors.

COHOST: The operational question is not only deportation. It is what happens to families, employers, schools, churches, and legal-aid groups before and after the appointment.

HOST: Exactly. T P S allowed many Haitians to work legally and avoid deportation because Haiti was considered too dangerous for forced return. After recent court action cleared the way for the Trump administration to end those protections, people who built ordinary work and family routines are facing status loss, work-permit disruption, monitoring, detention risk, and legal confusion.

HOST: The counter-signal is that a summons is not automatically a detention order. Some people may leave with another date, an ankle monitor, or instructions rather than being held. But for Central Ohio, the near-term consequence is disruption risk. Employers may lose workers. Families may need child-care and document plans. Schools and churches may become support hubs. Legal clinics may be overwhelmed by people who need individualized advice quickly.

HOST: Watch Wednesday for what people actually leave with: detention, monitoring, court dates, removal instructions, or clearer guidance. Also watch whether Columbus, Westerville, Springfield, employers, and state officials ask for more transparent federal procedures.

Mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury directionally, but they are also shaped by mortgage-backed security spreads, lender capacity, inflation expectations, servicing value, and borrower pull-through risk.

For people near the edge of qualifying, rerun approval math before they make offers. For refinance prospects, define a real trigger rate and break-even point. For sales managers, separate the pipeline into still-qualified buyers, concession-dependent buyers, rate-sensitive refinance leads, and borrowers who should wait before another credit pull.

The listener takeaway is that softer labor may create a pricing window, but it is not the same as a new low-rate regime.

HOST: There is also a mortgage A I governance issue that should not get buried. Fannie Mae's A I and machine-learning governance framework is now effective for seller-servicers. Lenders using A I in origination or servicing need policies, named ownership, risk controls, information-security alignment, vendor oversight, and the ability to explain what they use, why they use it, how they use it, and what safeguards are in place.

COHOST: So the useful test is evidence. Can the lender map the tool to a loan process and show the human control point?

HOST: Exactly. A document-intelligence tool, servicing chatbot, borrower-conversion assistant, quality-control model, fraud screen, pricing model, or staff copilot needs an owner, access boundary, audit trail, monitoring plan, vendor contract language, and escalation path. The counter-signal is that Fannie Mae is not banning A I. It is making undocumented A I harder to defend. Watch whether investors, warehouse banks, and auditors start asking for model inventories and workflow-level controls.

HOST: In A I and technology, the latest model story remains OpenAI's Astra disclosure, but the fresh enterprise angle is broader than a single release delay. OpenAI said Friday that internal evaluations of its upcoming Astra model showed enough agentic coding and cybersecurity progress that the company cannot rule out critical cyber capabilities under its Preparedness Framework. Axios followed today by framing the pause as part of a larger industry problem: advanced A I systems are becoming more useful and harder to contain at the same time.

COHOST: For executives, that means the model choice is only half the decision. The operating environment is the other half.

HOST: Exactly. OpenAI says the critical cyber threshold covers models that could identify and develop functional zero-day exploits across hardened real-world systems, or execute novel end-to-end cyberattack strategies from high-level goals. The company says Astra is upcoming, not public, and was not involved in exploiting Hugging Face. It also says it is using stricter controls: isolated testing environments, restricted network and tool access, stronger model-weight protections, monitoring, sandboxed execution, and a pause on internal Astra activities that do not meet the new requirements.

HOST: The affected parties are software companies, banks, mortgage firms, insurers, hospitals, government agencies, cyber teams, vendors, and customers whose data could be exposed through poorly governed agents. The practical consequence is an A I control stack. If an agent can browse, write code, use tools, open tickets, inspect customer records, send messages, or touch repositories, it needs a registry, named owner, least-privilege access, approved tool list, sandbox, logging, human approval for external actions, cost limits, incident response, and a kill switch.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that preliminary evaluations are not proof of public-world harm. They are a warning about capability under test conditions.

HOST: Right. But that is when governance is cheapest. The latest-model takeaway for regulated firms is model routing plus private evaluation. Use higher-capability models where reasoning, coding, document review, or cyber defense truly needs them. Use cheaper models for routine volume. Keep sensitive data behind permission boundaries. And measure workflows against business outcomes, not demo quality.

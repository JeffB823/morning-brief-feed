HOST: The domestic economic story is the jobs setup. ADP reported Wednesday that private-sector employers added only 38,000 jobs in August, down from a revised 46,000 in July and below expectations. ADP's pay data showed base pay up 3.2 percent year over year and gross pay up 4.7 percent.

COHOST: That is a low-hiring signal, but not automatically a recession signal.

HOST: Correct. The labor market still looks more like no hire, no fire than broad layoffs. Recent jobless-claims data have stayed near historically low levels, and the unemployment rate has been about 4.1 percent. The issue is mobility. If employers are slow to hire and workers are slow to quit, job seekers feel stuck even when layoffs are not surging.

HOST: The practical consequence for the Fed is uncomfortable. Weak hiring would normally argue for rate patience. But oil, tariffs, and service inflation make it harder to declare victory. Markets are expecting Friday's official payroll report to show modest job growth, around the low tens of thousands, with unemployment near 4.1 percent. If the number is weak and wage growth cools, the 10-year Treasury could fall and give mortgage rates a little relief before the Fed does anything. If payrolls surprise higher while oil stays elevated, the September 15th and 16th F O M C meeting remains a live inflation-management problem.

COHOST: So the watch item is not just the payroll number. It is whether the report changes the mix of growth risk and inflation risk.

HOST: In Central Ohio, the freshest local story is Grove City's data-center fight. Axios Columbus reports Grove City is debating a proposed charter amendment tied to a planned 300-acre, 6.9 billion dollar data-center project. City Council has already passed a one-year moratorium on data-center development, but the ballot proposal would go further by adding limits and requirements that opponents say could affect not only new projects but parts of the existing business base.

COHOST: This is a local-control story with a very practical infrastructure bill attached.

HOST: Exactly. Supporters organized as Protect Grove City argue residents need a stronger voice before a huge data-center project reshapes land use, utility demand, traffic, noise, water use, and community character. Opponents, including economic-development voices, warn the amendment could damage Grove City's tax base and make the city look unpredictable to employers.

HOST: The practical consequence is that data centers force cities to choose what kind of growth they want and what terms they can enforce. A project measured in hundreds of acres and billions of dollars can bring tax revenue and construction work, but it can also consume power capacity, require substations and transmission upgrades, affect road planning, and create relatively few permanent jobs compared with the land and utility load. For residents, the question is whether promised revenue translates into better services, lower pressure on other taxpayers, or infrastructure improvements they can actually see.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that a ballot amendment can be too blunt a tool for technical land-use policy.

HOST: Right. A moratorium gives officials time to write zoning, utility, environmental, and community-benefit rules. A charter amendment can be harder to fix if language creates unintended consequences. Axios reports a compromise version has been floated, including changing a 50-acre cap to a square-foot limit and removing retroactive provisions, but it may be too late to replace the original ballot language. Watch whether city leaders publish clearer fiscal and utility numbers, whether the compromise gains real traction, and whether other Central Ohio suburbs copy the approach.

HOST: The other Columbus watch item is civic process. City Council has until Friday, September 4th, to advance ballot language for competing council-election measures. Our City, Our Say qualified a proposal that would let only district residents vote for their district council member. Council leaders have considered a competing alternative that could preserve broader at-large influence.

COHOST: That could leave voters choosing not only representatives, but the structure of representation itself.

HOST: Exactly. The affected parties are voters, neighborhood groups, candidates, donors, developers, transit advocates, housing advocates, and anyone trying to influence city priorities. Ward-only elections can lower campaign costs and sharpen district accountability. At-large voting can be defended as a way to force members to consider citywide issues like housing supply, water, public safety, transit, and infrastructure. The uncertainty is whether competing measures would confuse voters or create legal conflict if both pass. Watch the exact ballot language by tomorrow and whether the city explains plainly what happens if two versions win.

HOST: In home lending and mortgage, the fresh signal is mixed, and that is why it is useful. M B A's weekly survey for the week ending August 28th showed total mortgage applications up 0.8 percent. HousingWire reports the adjustable-rate mortgage share rose to 8 percent. But Fannie Mae's application-level data for the same week showed purchase applications down 0.9 percent, total refinance application volume down 1.1 percent, rate-term refinance down 2 percent, and cash-out refinance down 0.4 percent. Year over year, Fannie Mae's rate-term refinance index was down 41.8 percent while cash-out refinance was up 2.7 percent.

COHOST: So one survey says activity ticked up, but the borrower mix still looks strained.

HOST: Exactly. Freddie Mac's latest published survey still had the 30-year fixed mortgage at 6.66 percent as of August 27th and the 15-year at 5.98 percent. Freddie Mac releases the next survey at noon today. The operational takeaway is that lenders should not treat a small weekly application gain as a broad demand recovery. A R M share rising says some borrowers are trying to manufacture affordability. Fannie Mae's split says rate-term refinance is still hard to make work unless a borrower is clearly in the money after costs.

HOST: The practical consequence for loan officers is segmentation. Purchase borrowers need advice around payment shock, seller credits, builder buydowns, property taxes, insurance, debt cleanup, and whether an adjustable product is genuinely suitable. Refinance teams should separate cash-out borrowers solving liquidity or debt-consolidation problems from rate-term borrowers who need a durable payment improvement. Servicers should watch for retention risk if the jobs report pulls rates down, but they should avoid generic refinance messaging that wastes trust.

COHOST: The counter-signal is that Friday's payroll report can move mortgage pricing faster than the weekly surveys can capture.

HOST: Right. Watch today's Freddie Mac update, the 10-year Treasury, mortgage-backed securities spreads, and whether lenders change borrower communication from rate headlines to use-case-specific advice.

HOST: In A I and technology, the newest model signal comes from Google. Google announced Gemini 3.8 Flash and Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber on Wednesday. The company says 3.8 Flash is built for long-horizon coding and autonomous agents, with substantial gains from 3.7 Flash. The Cyber version is restricted to trusted defenders through Google's Fairwind Program and is designed for vulnerability discovery and automated patching.

COHOST: That pairs directly with OpenAI's Astra story from yesterday.

HOST: It does. OpenAI says Astra is the first OpenAI model to meet its critical cybersecurity capability threshold under the Preparedness Framework, meaning that with the right tools and access it can find previously unknown security flaws and develop ways to exploit them across well-protected systems without a person guiding each step. Google frames Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber more defensively, emphasizing CodeMender, patch generation, and trusted access. But the enterprise implication is similar: frontier models are now practical cyber actors inside the operating environment.

HOST: The affected parties are banks, mortgage companies, cloud providers, software teams, security operations centers, regulators, insurers, and vendors. For a large enterprise, the question is not just which model is smarter. It is which model can touch production code, customer data, internal systems, ticketing queues, developer tools, browsers, and cloud credentials.

COHOST: The practical best practice is an implementation system, not a model leaderboard.

HOST: Exactly. Mature companies need an agent registry, named owners, approved tool scopes, private evaluations, logging, human approval points, cost controls, access boundaries, and a kill switch. For home lending, that matters before using agents for document review, loan-condition clearing, quality control, fraud triage, servicing chat, borrower outreach, secondary-market analytics, or staff copilots. The productivity upside is real: faster code review, faster vulnerability fixes, faster document intelligence, and better support capacity. The counter-signal is that each gain expands the blast radius if identity, permissions, monitoring, and vendor oversight are weak.

HOST: The latest-models takeaway is that the race is moving from general chat quality to agentic work under constraint. Google's pitch is speed and cost for coding and cyber defense. OpenAI's pitch is acknowledging critical capability and gating access. Anthropic's recent Claude Fable and Mythos updates add the same theme from another angle: stronger coding, research, and specialized high-risk capability with tighter safeguards. Watch which vendors provide evidence that their models improve real enterprise metrics without hiding the control model.

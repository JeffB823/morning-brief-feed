CHAPTER: Columbus Business
HOST: Columbus-based Huntington announced Sunday that small-business owners can sign up for early access to its 1866 Studio, an AI-powered service built with Google Cloud’s Gemini platform. Huntington describes a connected place for business guidance, financial information, digital tools and human expertise. The change today is an early-access invitation, not proof that agents are already completing customer work at scale.
COHOST: What task is this meant to improve for a small-business owner who already has banking and accounting tools?
HOST: Huntington says the service is intended to help owners monitor business operations and finances, manage daily work, and move from guidance toward action. That is a broad product promise. A useful test will be whether a particular owner can complete a defined task—such as organizing cash information or finding the right expert—more accurately and quickly than with the existing separate tools.
HOST: The local consequence is that a Columbus institution is making a customer-facing experiment visible beyond a lab or employee pilot. But early access means the audience, features, reliability and pricing may still change. Huntington says owners do not have to be customers to join the waitlist, so the next meaningful disclosure is which workflows enter general availability and how the bank evaluates errors or abandoned tasks.
COHOST: That makes the right question less about whether the interface sounds intelligent, and more about whether an owner can finish a real decision without being sent in circles.
HOST: Precisely. For a local entrepreneur, the sensible comparison is the current path: spreadsheets, website searches, a banker and existing software. If the new service reduces handoffs while preserving a route to a knowledgeable person, that is tangible. If it only produces generic suggestions, it may add another screen without removing any work.
CHAPTER: Home Lending
HOST: Fannie Mae began adding multiple-credit-score fields to its single-family mortgage-backed-security disclosure files for October-issued securities on October nineteenth. The operational change affects the data investors and analytics teams receive after loans are pooled: the files add Fannie Score, Freddie Score, pricing-indicator and weighted-average fields alongside information on the score models used in pricing.
COHOST: Is that a new borrower underwriting rule starting this morning?
HOST: No. This is a securities-disclosure change, not a promise that a borrower’s approval criteria changed today. It supports the broader transition to multiple score models by making pool information more visible to market participants. A lender should not turn a new file field into a file-specific eligibility assurance without the applicable selling guidance and its own investor instructions.
HOST: The affected workflow is post-issuance data handling. An investor downloading a PoolTalk file, a capital-markets team comparing pools, or a vendor mapping the feed needs to recognize the new attributes rather than misread them as missing data or an unexpected pricing anomaly. Fannie Mae says securities issued earlier in October will also be updated with the new attributes.
COHOST: What is the practical risk if systems treat that as a cosmetic format change? A capital-markets team may see an unexpected value and label it an exception, while an investor’s analytic model may accept the file but place the new information in the wrong category. Both errors can be quiet until someone uses the resulting report.
HOST: A report can silently compare unlike fields, or a downstream model can reject a file it was never configured to parse. The immediate action is a controlled file-mapping check: identify the added columns, test historical-file handling, and confirm what each score-related field represents. That is different from changing an underwriting threshold, and it is why the effective date matters.
COHOST: The unresolved question is how consistently market participants interpret the attributes across issuers and their own analytics. Watch for updated technical documentation, vendor release notes, and any investor feedback after the October files circulate. Rate context stays secondary here: this is about mortgage-market data plumbing, not a daily rate quote.
CHAPTER: AI in Banking
HOST: Santander announced Monday a new three-year NVIDIA collaboration framework and described work in its AI Lab to evaluate models and agents for banking tasks. I added a new lane-seven and lane-one ledger row: the reported status is experimental, not a production rollout across the bank. The named task is selecting a suitable model for a banking application and testing customer-service conversations.
COHOST: Did the bank disclose a result, or only say that it intends to experiment?
HOST: It disclosed both a broad program and selected experimental figures. Santander says a customer-service use case improved conversation quality while cutting costs by more than half, and says some specialized-model work moved serving cost from about five euros per interaction to about three cents while maintaining strong performance. Those are bank claims, not an independent test or a measured customer-outcome study.
COHOST: What baseline is missing that a large bank would need before treating those figures as a budget case? It needs a defined set of comparable customer conversations, an agreed quality score, and a record of where people were escalated or corrected. Without those, an apparent saving could be a change in what the system chooses not to handle.
HOST: The announcement does not specify the conversation-quality measure, the task mix, traffic volume, error rate, or how human escalation changed. That matters because a low-cost answer is not a win if it sends a payment dispute or vulnerable customer to the wrong path. The appropriate comparison is the earlier model and human-assisted process on the same defined conversations, including corrections and transfers.
HOST: For a large bank, this changes the vendor-choice question: model routing may be a cost and performance decision by task, rather than a single enterprise-model decision. The relevant control is a test set that includes high-consequence cases before a smaller model is allowed to handle them. Santander’s disclosure is promising implementation evidence, but the bank has not provided enough detail for an outside team to reproduce the savings.
COHOST: In other words, the usable lesson is to measure a particular workflow, not to assume that a cheaper model makes every banking interaction cheaper in practice.
HOST: Exactly. The next evidence to seek is a deployment scope, an independently auditable quality measure, or a result that reports escalation and error alongside cost. That would move this ledger entry from a well-described experiment toward a more decision-ready comparison.
CHAPTER: Frontier AI
HOST: MiniMax released M3.1 Flash on Monday, positioning it for agent tasks, coding, computer use and cybersecurity. This clears the frontier tracker’s price-and-access bar because a newly available model changes the cost and speed proposition for those workflows. The lab says online inference is nearly three times faster and agent usage costs seventy percent less than its prior model.
COHOST: Are those numbers a measured third-party result, or MiniMax’s own performance claim?
HOST: They are MiniMax’s claims in its launch material. The company says the model achieves its improvement through its approach to efficiency, but the release is not an independent replication on a bank’s software, documents or security controls. A purchaser should treat the figures as a reason to test the new version, not as evidence that a particular internal agent will cost seventy percent less.
HOST: The capability change is availability of a Flash variant aimed at repeated tool calls, where latency and per-call expense can accumulate. The tradeoff is that faster, lower-cost inference says little by itself about reliability on a long multi-step task. An agent can return an answer swiftly and still choose the wrong tool, mishandle a state change, or require more human review.
COHOST: What is the fair baseline for deciding whether the release really changes a workflow?
HOST: Run the same task sequence against the prior model with identical tools, prompts and success checks. Measure total completion cost, time, correction rate and failures—not just tokens per second. That separates a lab’s throughput claim from an operational outcome, and it tells an engineering buyer whether the lower price survives the work around the model.
HOST: A regulated bank could first use M3.1 Flash in a contained engineering evaluation, gated by task-level accuracy and security approval before it accesses production systems.
